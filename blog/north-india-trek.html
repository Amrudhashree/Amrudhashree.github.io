Travel Story and Beginner Guide

From Warm Coimbatore to Freezing Himalayan Peaks: My First North India Trek

By Amrudha Shree · Trip Date: May 2023 · Route: Coimbatore, Delhi, Chopta, Chandrashila, Deoriatal, Rishikesh

Have you ever booked a trip that made your stomach flip with excitement and pure fear at the exact same time? That was me in May 2023 when my husband and I planned our first North India trek from Coimbatore to the Garhwal Himalayas.

Living in warm South India, I had never experienced mountain snow, freezing mountain wind, or a steep multi-day climb. Every part of this trip was a first for me.

Preparing in Coimbatore Before Facing Real Mountain Cold

Good preparation turns panic into calm confidence. About a month before leaving Coimbatore, my husband and I walked into Decathlon to buy winter trekking gear for the first time in our lives. I picked up sturdy waterproof trekking boots, thermal innerwear, a warm fleece sweater, a teal padded winter jacket, gloves, and a trekking pole.

I tried on those boots at home and wondered if my legs could carry me up steep Himalayan trails. I had seen photos of snowy ridges, yet no screen prepares you for how cold mountain air really feels. We packed warm layers, two liters of water capacity, oral rehydration packets, sunscreen, lip balm, and basic pain and fever tablets. Looking out through the cabin window on our flight to Delhi, I knew there was no turning back.

A Hectic 450 km Overnight Ride to Devprayag Sangam and Dhari Devi Mandir

How fast can scenery change in one night? At 10 PM in Delhi, a tempo traveller picked us up near Mayur Vihar for an overnight drive of nearly 450 kilometers into Uttarakhand. For twelve to thirteen hours, flat city roads gave way to winding river valleys as morning light broke over the hills. It was a hectic ride with little sleep, yet the mountains kept us wide awake.

Our first major stop was Devprayag Sangam, the final and most famous of the five sacred river confluences known as the Panch Prayag. Here, the earthy brown Alaknanda River flowing from Satopanth Glacier meets the clear green-blue Bhagirathi River from Gangotri Glacier to form the holy River Ganga. Watching two distinct water colors flow side by side before slowly blending into one strong current woke me up faster than any alarm clock.

Further up the highway near Srinagar Garhwal, we stopped at Dhari Devi Mandir on the Alaknanda River. The temple rests on elevated concrete pillars right above the water, linked to the riverbank by a pedestrian walkway. Dhari Devi is revered as a form of Goddess Kali and the guardian deity of Uttarakhand and the Char Dham shrines. The lower half of her sacred idol is worshipped miles away at Kalimath. Local belief holds that the goddess shifts appearance across morning, afternoon, and evening. Standing quietly over the rushing river made the long overnight drive worth every stiff kilometer.

Reaching Chopta Camp, 2°C Air, and Lifesaving Hot Coffee

By afternoon, we climbed into Chopta, a high mountain meadow area sitting around 2,680 meters above sea level inside the Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary. Travelers often call Chopta the Mini Switzerland of Uttarakhand because wide green slopes open straight toward forested ridges and distant peaks. Coming from May heat in Coimbatore, stepping out of the van felt like opening a freezer door.

We checked into a Swiss tent campsite near Chopta, complete with proper beds, heavy blankets, and attached washrooms. Outside, the temperature hovered near 2°C, and thick clouds settled low over the ridge. Cold air crept straight through thin fabric whenever the breeze picked up.

Inside a small tin-walled roadside stall, two simple things rescued our afternoon. Steaming hot coffee in paper cups and a hot bowl of Maggi noodles brought feeling right back into my numb fingers. Food never tastes better than when your teeth are chattering in 2°C mountain mist.

Climbing Toward Chandrashila Peak When Breathing Got Hard

Early next morning, the Chopta trailhead buzzed with pilgrims, local guides, and fellow trekkers pulling on jackets and woollen caps. Standing below the red brick entrance arch felt exciting and intimidating all at once. Before starting the climb, I reached up to ring one of the heavy brass bells hanging under the archway. That tiny chime felt like making a quiet promise to finish what we started.

Just past the archway, a bright blue welcome board greeted trekkers as the concrete ramp sloped upward into the shaded woods. The initial path climbs along paved stones under a thick canopy of oak and rhododendron trees. Right away, I noticed how busy the route was. Groups of younger hikers and local pilgrims moved past us at a brisk pace, talking easily while climbing uphill.

I did not match their speed, and for a brief moment I felt embarrassed. Within an hour, my lungs burned in the thin air and my breath came in short gasps. My husband reminded me that a mountain climb works only when you listen to your own body.

A mountain climb is not a race against strangers. Slowing down to catch my breath and leaning on my trekking pole kept me moving upward when pride might have forced me to quit.

Once we rose above the treeline, the weather changed fast. Sudden rain swept across the ridge, followed by sharp bursts of hail that stung our cheeks as the temperature dropped close to 0°C. Green alpine grass turned patchy white with ice and leftover snow, while mountain wind tested every thermal layer I had packed in Coimbatore. Looking up the slope, the trail wound along the dark rocky ridge like a thin ribbon filled with determined hikers.

Higher up the ridge, white patches of snow clung beside small cairns while layered blue mountain ranges stretched into the clouds behind us. Beneath us, golden-green high meadows called bugyals rolled smoothly toward deep wooded valleys. For someone raised near warm southern plains, seeing mountain ridges stack one behind another felt almost unreal.

Standing on Chandrashila Peak With Stacked Stones and Silent Prayers

What keeps your feet lifting when every calf muscle screams to stop? Every ten steps, I told myself just reach the next rock turn, and then one more turn after that. Pausing briefly on a stone ledge to catch my breath above the clouds, I looked back at how far we had already climbed.

When the steep path finally flattened into a windy summit crest, I stood at Chandrashila Peak, rising roughly 4,000 meters above sea level. Chandrashila translates to Moon Rock, and local legends connect this summit both to the moon god Chandra and to Lord Rama meditating here in quiet penance after the war in the Ramayana.

Across the flat grey rock, trekkers had carefully balanced hundreds of flat stones into little towers as silent prayers for strength and gratitude. I stood beside those stone stacks with snow-covered Himalayan peaks rising behind me, grinning behind my sunglasses because I had actually made it. Beside one of the tallest balanced stone towers on the peak, I leaned on my blue trekking pole and soaked in the crisp summit air. Every heavy breath on the climb suddenly made total sense.

Looking down from the high crest, the temple spur and steep meadow trails below looked like a miniature world dotted with colorful jackets. On a clearing sky across the horizon, layers of snow peaks stretched farther than my eyes could measure. On clear days from Chandrashila, travelers spot towering Garhwal peaks including Chaukhamba, Nanda Devi, Trishul, and Kedar Dome rising above deep dark valleys. Even with clouds drifting across the highest ridges, the wide silence made every breathless hour on the climb feel worth it.

Near the rocky crest, we paused by a small shrine built from stacked flat stones, marked by a bright red cloth and tiny brass bell in the doorway. At the summit sits a modest Ganga shrine known locally alongside Ravan Shila, surrounded by wind-worn rock cairns built by visitors from across India. No fancy marble walls, just mountain slate holding centuries of faith in freezing wind. Before turning back down, I walked a few more steps along the narrow ridge path toward the snow-capped horizon, holding my trekking pole firmly against the cold breeze.

Tungnath Temple and the Quiet Descent Through Pine Woods

Heading down from Chandrashila, we reached the green entrance archway welcoming trekkers to Jai Baba Tungnath. Clouds floated low past the snow ridges as pilgrims queued quietly along the stone terrace. Tungnath Temple sits at around 3,680 meters above sea level. It is recognized as the highest Shiva temple in the world and the third shrine in the sacred Panch Kedar circuit, traditionally linked to the Pandava brothers. Standing before its weathered grey stone structure while clouds drifted past felt grounding and peaceful.

Descending below the temple ridge, afternoon sun broke through the clouds. I sat briefly on a warm granite boulder with snow-covered peaks rising behind pine trees, finally relaxing my tired shoulders. Lower down, the walk led us back through open green slopes and tall coniferous and oak woods where golden afternoon light filtered between thick moss-covered trunks. Resting briefly against an ancient tree trunk with my trekking pole in hand, I felt tired to the bone yet calmer than I had felt in months.

Before reaching the valley bottom, I lifted my camera one more time among fallen oak leaves to capture the evening light slanting through twisted tree branches. By the time we walked back down through the Chopta entrance, my legs felt heavy and shaky, and sleep came easily under thick camp blankets.

Sore Muscles, Persistence, and a Hand to Hold on the Deoriatal Climb

Nobody warns you properly about morning number two on a beginner trek. When I woke up, every muscle in my calves, thighs, and shoulders ached sharply. Part of me wanted to stay wrapped in warm blankets instead of stepping onto another uphill path. That morning taught me what persistence actually looks like. Motivation gets you onto a flight, while quiet stubbornness gets you out of bed when your legs hurt. We drove to Sari Village, a terraced farming settlement around 2,000 meters high that serves as the gateway for the Deoriatal Lake trek inside Kedarnath Wildlife Sanctuary.

As we climbed higher, dense mountain forest closed in overhead and cool mist replaced the bright morning sun. While guidebooks often list the roughly three-kilometer climb to Deoriatal at under three hours, our sore legs and the steep stone steps stretched the climb to four full hours.

Real strength does not mean walking alone without help. When my legs felt weakest halfway up the trail, reaching out and holding a steady hand carried me farther than pride ever could.

Halfway up, when my soreness peaked and the rocky steps felt endless, my husband walked ahead and reached back to hold my hand tightly. That simple grip on a rough dirt trail meant more to me than any pep talk. Slowing down on that four-hour climb gave us a small gift fast hikers missed. Perched quietly among fresh green leaves above the trail sat a striking Black-headed Jay, showing off its black crest, streaked throat, and bright blue-and-black barred wing feathers. Moments like spotting mountain birds up close happen only when you slow your pace enough to look around.

After four steady hours of uphill grit, the dense forest finally opened onto a peaceful grassy clearing cradling Deoriatal Lake at about 2,438 meters above sea level. Legend says the gods bathed in these waters and links the lake to the Mahabharata story where the Pandavas answered the Yaksha's questions. Even without knowing the old stories, seeing still emerald water surrounded by silent green hills makes your tired shoulders drop at once. I walked down to the shoreline and sat on an old weathered log stretching right over the shallow edge. Looking across the water with wind cooling my cheeks, my sore muscles faded into genuine pride.

Heading back down toward Sari Village under gathering grey clouds, we passed an ancient stone mountain temple where yellow flags snapped lightly in the breeze and hikers rested on stone benches along the quiet hillside.

Facing Fear in Rishikesh: 16 km Rafting Without Knowing How to Swim

From Sari Village, we drove roughly 200 kilometers down toward Rishikesh, trading chilly pine air for warm river breezes. The next adventure on the schedule was a 16-kilometer white-water rafting run down the Ganga, the popular Shivpuri stretch known for Grade II and Grade III rapids like Roller Coaster, Golf Course, and Return to Sender.

Here is the honest truth: I resisted getting onto that raft with everything I had. I had read scary news stories about flash floods and careless river operators. On top of that, neither my husband nor I knew how to swim. Watching my husband's calm excitement, though, made me pause and trust him. Instead of letting fear decide for me, I strapped on a blue helmet and red life jacket on the sandy riverbank and listened carefully to every safety rule our guides shouted out.

Their clear, confident briefing settled my racing heart right away. I assumed we would simply sit inside the yellow raft and paddle through foamy waves for two or three hours. I did not expect what happened once the raft reached a wide, calmer bend of the river. With cheerful grins, the guides suddenly pushed us into the river water so we could float and swim in the gentle current. For one second, icy cold shock hit my chest and every non-swimmer instinct screamed. Right away, the snug life jacket lifted me safely to the surface. Cool Ganga water refreshed my tired body, panic turned into laughter, and I felt truly empowered after stepping straight past my fear.

Fear shrinks the moment you meet it head on. Floating safely down a cold Himalayan river without knowing how to swim taught me to trust preparation instead of panic.

That evening, we walked along the riverfront ghats near Ram Jhula and Lakshman Jhula to soak in the calmer side of Rishikesh. From an overlook above the river at dusk, we watched the orange towers and glowing cables of Ram Jhula light up against green hills. Down on the stone ghat steps as blue twilight deepened, warm golden lights along Ram Jhula shimmered across the dark river water while travelers sat quietly taking in the evening calm.

Flying Back to Coimbatore With a Stronger Mind

Soon enough, we took the overnight drive back to Delhi and boarded our evening flight home to Coimbatore. Looking out at a glowing orange sunset layered above dark clouds, I realized this trip gave me far more than mountain photos. I left Coimbatore unsure whether cold weather, steep stone trails, or rushing river water would defeat me. I came home with four steady lessons that still guide me today:

Four steady takeaways
  1. 01 Preparation lowers fear.
  2. 02 Slow steps still reach high summits.
  3. 03 Accepting a helping hand is real strength.
  4. 04 Courage does not mean feeling zero fear. It means stepping forward with shaky knees anyway and discovering how capable you really are.
For fellow beginners

Quick Practical Guide for First-Time Himalayan Trekkers

If you are planning a similar beginner trip from South India or anywhere else, here is what worked cleanly for us in late May.

Our Route Summary

  • Start and End: Coimbatore to Delhi by flight, then overnight tempo traveller road trip into Uttarakhand
  • Highway Stops: Devprayag Sangam confluence and Dhari Devi Mandir along the Alaknanda River
  • Base Camp & Summit: Chopta Swiss tents, Tungnath Temple, and Chandrashila Peak
  • Day Three Climb: Drive to Sari Village and trek up to Deoriatal Lake
  • Final Adventure: Drive 200 km to Rishikesh for 16 km Shivpuri rafting and evening walks near Ram Jhula

May Weather Reality

  • Lower River Valleys: Daytime in Rishikesh and Devprayag feels warm and breezy
  • Camp Nights: Temperatures around Chopta drop close to 2°C once sun disappears or clouds move in
  • Summit Ridges: High exposed slopes toward Chandrashila can drop near 0°C with sudden rain, wind, or hail even in late May
